Grade 6 Earth and Space Science — Water Shapes Land

📚 Grade: 6th Grade⏱️ Duration: 40–55 minutes🔬 Subject: Earth and Space Science — Earth Systems
An independent investigation: plan carefully, collect evidence, and let your results do some arguing.
📋 NGSS Standard:
MS-ESS2-2 — Construct an explanation based on evidence for how geoscience processes have changed Earth’s surface at varying time and spatial scales.

🎯 Learning Objective

Students will plan and conduct a fair investigation, organize evidence, and use scientific reasoning to explain a pattern in their results.

💡 The Big Idea

Water can move sediment and reshape land. Ground cover can change how rain hits soil, how fast water runs, and how much sediment is carried away.

🔬 The Science

Three linked words

Weathering breaks rock or soil into smaller pieces. Erosion moves those pieces. Deposition drops them when moving water loses energy.

Why cover can help

Leaves, mulch, and plant roots can soften raindrop impact, slow runoff, and help hold soil in place. A simple tray is a model, not a whole watershed.

Model limits

Models let scientists isolate one cause-and-effect relationship. Your conclusion should describe the tray conditions, then explain carefully what may transfer to a real landscape.

🧪 Let’s Get Started

Your investigation question
How does ground cover affect the amount or pattern of sediment moved by water?
Independent variable: ground-cover treatment
Dependent variable: sediment collected, channel width, or a defined runoff rating
Controls: soil amount, tray slope, water volume, pour height, storm time
Step 1 — Build two equal soil slopes in a tray or two matching trays: bare soil is your control; cover the second with one material.
Step 2 — Choose one evidence measure before testing.
Explain why it answers your question.
Step 3 — Pour the same measured “rainstorm” from the same height onto each slope for the same time.
Collect runoff if possible.
Step 4 — Repeat the storm.
Record sediment movement, channels, and where deposition occurs.
Step 5 — Graph or rank your evidence, then write a recommendation for a landscape manager using your data.

💬 Discussion Questions

Which process moved sediment in this model?Erosion moved it. Deposition happened where moving water slowed and dropped sediment.
Why is bare soil a useful control?It gives a comparison point so the effect of cover is easier to identify.
How might steepness change the result?A steeper slope can increase runoff speed and carrying ability; students should test rather than assume exact amounts.
What does your model leave out?Real landscapes have roots, rainfall variation, soil types, channels, wind, and much larger scale.
Did ground cover reduce erosion, increase deposition, or both?A careful answer separates less sediment leaving the slope from sediment merely settling sooner.

🚀 Extensions

Stretch your thinking: Test an interaction: Does your ground cover work differently on a steeper slope or during a larger storm? Track sediment from source to final location and make a mass-balance argument about erosion versus deposition.

👩‍🏫 Parent / Teacher Notes

Keep the independence real: Give the student the question, safety boundaries, and materials; let them make the prediction, decide a reasonable measurement, and defend their controls. Ask “What evidence makes you say that?” before supplying an answer.

✂️ PARENT ANSWER KEY — Detach before giving worksheet to students

Expected results

Ground cover often reduces visible channels and sediment in runoff, but results depend on soil, slope, cover type, and water delivery. Accept a conclusion that accurately reports the student’s evidence and model limits.

What mastery looks like

  • Identifies a testable variable and protects meaningful controls.
  • Records repeated observations or measurements accurately.
  • Uses a graph or table to state a claim.
  • Connects evidence to the scientific principle instead of only describing what happened.

Troubleshooting

If results are inconsistent, check whether the student changed more than one variable, used unequal starting materials, or measured at different times. A surprising result is not a failed experiment; repeat it and improve the test.
