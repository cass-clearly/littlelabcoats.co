🥔 Potato Osmosis Lab 🥔

Grade 6 Life Science — Cell Membranes and Water Movement

📚 Grade: 6th Grade⏱️ Duration: 30 minutes + 24-hour observation🔬 Subject: Life Science — Cells and Systems
An independent investigation: plan carefully, collect evidence, and let your results do some arguing.
📋 NGSS Standard:
MS-LS1-2 — Develop and use a model to describe the function of a cell as a whole way and ways parts of cells contribute to the function.

🎯 Learning Objective

Students will plan and conduct a fair investigation, organize evidence, and use scientific reasoning to explain a pattern in their results.

💡 The Big Idea

Plant-cell membranes let some substances cross more easily than others. Water can move across the membrane by osmosis, changing the firmness and mass of plant tissue.

🔬 The Science

Selective membrane

A cell membrane is not an open door. It controls movement into and out of the cell, helping keep conditions workable.

Osmosis

Water moves by osmosis toward the side with more dissolved particles. In a plant cell, water moving in can make the cell more turgid; water moving out can make it less firm.

A useful model

A potato strip contains many plant cells. It is not one giant cell, but changes in its mass or firmness give evidence about water movement across many cell membranes.

🧪 Let’s Get Started

Your investigation question
How does salt-solution concentration affect the mass or firmness of potato tissue?
Independent variable: salt-solution concentration
Dependent variable: change in mass (g) or length (mm), plus firmness
Controls: potato-strip size, solution volume, soak time, temperature
Step 1 — Ask an adult to cut equal potato strips.
Label cups with a control (plain water) and your chosen salt concentrations.
Step 2 — Measure and record each strip’s starting mass or length.
Make a prediction before the strips enter the cups.
Step 3 — Add the same volume of solution to every cup.
Place one strip in each cup and record the start time.
Step 4 — After at least 24 hours, blot each strip gently in the same way.
Measure again and record firmness observations.
Step 5 — Calculate change: after − before.
If using mass, calculate percent change and graph concentration against change.

💬 Discussion Questions

Which direction did water move in your plain-water condition?Often into the potato cells, if the strip gained mass or became firmer. Ask the student to connect their evidence to the claim.
Why must strips start the same size?Different-sized strips have different amounts of tissue and surface area, making the comparison unfair.
What does “more concentrated” mean?It means more dissolved solute particles in a given volume of solution.
Why record mass as well as firmness when possible?Mass gives a numerical measurement; firmness is useful qualitative evidence. Together they make a stronger case.
What result would make you revise your explanation?A trend that reverses, no pattern after careful trials, or conflicting measurements should prompt another test.

🚀 Extensions

Stretch your thinking: Calculate percent change in mass: (after − before) ÷ before × 100. Predict the approximate isotonic concentration, where there is little net water movement. Explain why percent change makes comparisons fairer when strips do not begin with exactly the same mass.

👩‍🏫 Parent / Teacher Notes

Keep the independence real: Give the student the question, safety boundaries, and materials; let them make the prediction, decide a reasonable measurement, and defend their controls. Ask “What evidence makes you say that?” before supplying an answer.

✂️ PARENT ANSWER KEY — Detach before giving worksheet to students

Expected results

In plain water, strips often gain mass or feel firmer. In more concentrated salt solutions, they often lose mass or feel softer. Real potatoes vary, so the student’s evidence and controls matter more than an exact number.

What mastery looks like

  • Identifies a testable variable and protects meaningful controls.
  • Records repeated observations or measurements accurately.
  • Uses a graph or table to state a claim.
  • Connects evidence to the scientific principle instead of only describing what happened.

Troubleshooting

If results are inconsistent, check whether the student changed more than one variable, used unequal starting materials, or measured at different times. A surprising result is not a failed experiment; repeat it and improve the test.
