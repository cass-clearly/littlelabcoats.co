Grade 6 Physical Science — Thermal Energy Transfer

📚 Grade: 6th Grade⏱️ Duration: 30–40 minutes🔬 Subject: Physical Science — Energy
An independent investigation: plan carefully, collect evidence, and let your results do some arguing.
📋 NGSS Standard:
MS-PS3-3 — Apply scientific principles to design, construct, and test a device that either minimizes or maximizes thermal energy transfer.

🎯 Learning Objective

Students will plan and conduct a fair investigation, organize evidence, and use scientific reasoning to explain a pattern in their results.

💡 The Big Idea

Thermal energy moves from warmer matter to cooler matter. Engineers choose materials and shapes that slow this transfer when they need to protect something cold.

🔬 The Science

Energy direction

Thermal energy transfers from warmer objects or surroundings to cooler ones. “Cold” does not flow out of an ice cube; energy moves into it.

Three pathways

Conduction transfers energy through contact. Convection transfers it through moving fluids such as air. Radiation transfers energy by electromagnetic waves.

Insulation

Insulators slow energy transfer, often because they trap still air. A good design has constraints: it must also fit, use available materials, and be testable fairly.

🧪 Let’s Get Started

Your investigation question
Which household insulation design best slows thermal energy transfer into an ice cube?
Independent variable: insulation material or design
Dependent variable: meltwater volume after a fixed time or remaining ice mass
Controls: ice size, cup size, location, timing, measurement method
Step 1 — Choose an uninsulated control and two or three insulation designs.
State one constraint: limited material, thickness, or cost.
Step 2 — Decide how you will measure melting.
Use equal ice cubes and identical cups where possible.
Step 3 — Build each prototype.
Start all timers together and keep the cups in the same location.
Step 4 — At the same time point, measure meltwater or remaining ice.
Repeat if time and ice allow.
Step 5 — Graph the results and explain which energy-transfer pathway each design may have slowed.

💬 Discussion Questions

Why is an uninsulated cup a control?It shows how the ice changes without the insulation treatment.
Which direction does thermal energy move here?From warmer surroundings into the colder ice and cup.
Why can trapped air be useful?Still air is a poor conductor and reduces convection compared with freely moving air.
Why might shiny foil help sometimes but not always?It can reflect radiant energy, but it may not prevent conduction or convection if it is in contact with the cup or has gaps.
What makes this engineering rather than only a science test?Students design within constraints, test a prototype, use evidence, and improve it.

🚀 Extensions

Stretch your thinking: Design a multi-layer prototype that reduces conduction, convection, and radiation at once. Defend trade-offs: thickness, material use, durability, and cost. Then predict which layer matters most and propose a test that isolates it.

👩‍🏫 Parent / Teacher Notes

Keep the independence real: Give the student the question, safety boundaries, and materials; let them make the prediction, decide a reasonable measurement, and defend their controls. Ask “What evidence makes you say that?” before supplying an answer.

✂️ PARENT ANSWER KEY — Detach before giving worksheet to students

Expected results

The best design is the one that produces the least meltwater or most remaining ice under the student’s controlled test. Results vary with cup shape, gaps, room temperature, and ice size.

What mastery looks like

  • Identifies a testable variable and protects meaningful controls.
  • Records repeated observations or measurements accurately.
  • Uses a graph or table to state a claim.
  • Connects evidence to the scientific principle instead of only describing what happened.

Troubleshooting

If results are inconsistent, check whether the student changed more than one variable, used unequal starting materials, or measured at different times. A surprising result is not a failed experiment; repeat it and improve the test.
