🚗 Friction Track Detective 🚗

Grade 6 Physical Science — Forces and Motion Investigation

📚 Grade: 6th Grade⏱️ Duration: 45–55 minutes🔬 Subject: Physical Science — Forces and Motion
An independent investigation: plan carefully, collect evidence, and let your results do some arguing.
📋 NGSS Standard:
MS-PS2-2 — Plan an investigation to provide evidence that the change in an object’s motion depends on the sum of the forces on the object and the mass of the object.

🎯 Learning Objective

Students will plan and conduct a fair investigation, organize evidence, and use scientific reasoning to explain a pattern in their results.

💡 The Big Idea

Friction is a contact force that pushes against motion. When the same toy car rolls across different surfaces, the surface can change how quickly the car slows down.

🔬 The Science

Force detective

A force is a push or pull. Forces can change speed or direction. On a flat track, friction acts opposite the car’s motion.

Why surfaces matter

Tiny bumps and stickiness at the contact surfaces make friction different. Rougher does not always mean “more friction” for every object, so your evidence matters.

Fair-test thinking

Change one variable: track surface. Keep the car, ramp height, release line, and measuring method the same. Repeated trials make a pattern more trustworthy.

🧪 Let’s Get Started

Your investigation question
How does track surface affect the distance a toy car travels after leaving the ramp?
Independent variable: track surface
Dependent variable: distance travelled after the ramp (cm)
Controls: car, ramp height, release line, measuring point
Step 1 — Mark one release line and one zero line with tape.
Choose three or four surfaces that fit the same track area.
Step 2 — Write a prediction and identify your independent variable, dependent variable, and at least three controls.
Step 3 — Release the car from the marked line without pushing.
Measure from the zero line to the stopping point.
Step 4 — Repeat three trials for each surface.
Calculate the mean distance for each surface.
Step 5 — Make a bar graph: surface on the x-axis; mean distance in centimetres on the y-axis.
Use your graph to revise or support your prediction.

💬 Discussion Questions

Which surface made the car stop soonest? What numerical evidence supports that?Look for an answer that names a surface and uses a mean or a clear comparison of the trials.
Why did we use the same ramp height and release line?They might say these controls keep the starting push from gravity as similar as possible.
Does the longest distance directly measure friction force?No. It is indirect evidence; other factors can affect distance, which is why controls and repeated trials matter.
What could make one trial an outlier?A crooked release, a bump, inconsistent surface placement, or a measurement error are all reasonable answers.
How could you improve this investigation?Look for more trials, a longer track, a spring scale, or a more controlled release.

🚀 Extensions

Stretch your thinking: Draw a free-body diagram for the car on the flat track: gravity, support force, motion direction, and friction. Then design a second version using a spring scale to measure the pulling force needed to keep the car moving at constant speed.

👩‍🏫 Parent / Teacher Notes

Keep the independence real: Give the student the question, safety boundaries, and materials; let them make the prediction, decide a reasonable measurement, and defend their controls. Ask “What evidence makes you say that?” before supplying an answer.

✂️ PARENT ANSWER KEY — Detach before giving worksheet to students

Expected results

The car will usually travel less far on a surface that produces more friction, but the exact ranking depends on the wheels and materials. A strong student conclusion uses their own mean values—not a prewritten “right” ranking.

What mastery looks like

  • Identifies a testable variable and protects meaningful controls.
  • Records repeated observations or measurements accurately.
  • Uses a graph or table to state a claim.
  • Connects evidence to the scientific principle instead of only describing what happened.

Troubleshooting

If results are inconsistent, check whether the student changed more than one variable, used unequal starting materials, or measured at different times. A surprising result is not a failed experiment; repeat it and improve the test.
